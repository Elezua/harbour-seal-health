Foundation

Nutritional Ecology & Biological Basis

How the natural feeding behaviour of harbour seals informs captive dietary management

Harbour seals (Phoca vitulina) are opportunistic generalist predators whose diet varies according to prey availability, habitat and seasonal fluctuations Luxa and Acevedo-Gutiérrez, 2013; Thomas et al., 2022. Across their geographical range, they consume a diverse range of pelagic and demersal fish, cephalopods and, to a lesser extent, crustaceans, demonstrating considerable dietary flexibility that enables them to exploit locally abundant prey resources Luxa and Acevedo-Gutiérrez, 2013; Thomas et al., 2022.

This ecological adaptability indicates that harbour seals have evolved to obtain nutrients from a variety of prey species rather than a single food source. Consequently, captive diets should aim to replicate this diversity while meeting nutritional requirements.

Key Insight

The dietary flexibility of wild harbour seals is a fundamental principle that should guide captive feeding programmes. Replicating natural prey diversity is essential for both nutritional adequacy and behavioural enrichment Gili, Meijer and Lacave, 2018.

Biological Adaptations

As obligate carnivores, harbour seals possess a simple monogastric stomach, a relatively short intestinal tract and limited hindgut fermentation, adaptations that enable efficient digestion of animal-derived proteins and lipids while providing minimal capacity to utilise plant material Gili, Meijer and Lacave, 2018.

These physiological characteristics make whole-prey diets biologically appropriate because they more closely resemble the nutritional composition of natural prey. Therefore, the formulation of captive diets should be guided by both the nutritional ecology and digestive adaptations of the species to support normal physiological function and promote long-term health.

Important Consideration

The monogastric digestive system of harbour seals means they cannot efficiently process plant-based foods. Diets must be composed entirely of animal-derived ingredients to meet their physiological needs Gili, Meijer and Lacave, 2018.

Selection

Selection & Justification of the Captive Diet

The four complementary prey species that form the foundation of a nutritionally balanced diet

Captive harbour seals are typically fed frozen-thawed whole fish and cephalopods, with Atlantic herring (Clupea harengus), Atlantic mackerel (Scomber scombrus), capelin (Mallotus villosus), smelt (Osmerus spp.) and squid among the most commonly used prey species in zoological collections Mazzaro, Koutsos and Williams, 2016; MSD Veterinary Manual, 2025. Whole-prey diets are preferred because they provide muscle, organs and skeletal tissues, supplying a broad spectrum of nutrients that closely resemble the composition of natural prey.

Current husbandry guidelines discourage reliance on a single prey species because the nutrient composition of marine fish varies considerably between species and seasons Gili, Meijer and Lacave, 2018. Both the MSD Veterinary Manual (2025) and EAZA/EAAM husbandry guidelines recommend offering a combination of high-fat and lean prey species to provide a balanced intake of protein, energy, essential fatty acids and micronutrients Gili, Meijer and Lacave, 2018.

Requirements

Daily Nutritional Requirements

Evidence-based feeding quantities for a healthy adult harbour seal under typical captive conditions

Harbour seals maintained in zoological collections typically consume 5–8% of their body weight daily, although requirements vary with age, reproductive status, activity level and environmental temperature MSD Veterinary Manual, 2025; Gili, Meijer and Lacave, 2018. For a healthy 75 kg adult, this equates to approximately 3.75–6.0 kg of whole prey per day. The proposed ration of approximately 4.5 kg/day provides around 29 MJ of metabolizable energy, meeting the estimated maintenance requirements of a healthy adult harbour seal under typical captive conditions Gili, Meijer and Lacave, 2018.

Diet formulation should be based on energy and nutrient provision rather than feed mass alone, as the energy density of herring, mackerel, squid and capelin varies according to species, season and lipid content Gili, Meijer and Lacave, 2018; Mazzaro, Koutsos and Williams, 2016.

Monitoring Over Fixed Quantities

As published nutrient requirements for harbour seals remain limited, dietary adequacy should be assessed through routine monitoring rather than fixed feeding quantities alone Gili, Meijer and Lacave, 2018. Regular evaluation of body weight, body condition score and feeding behaviour enables ration size and prey proportions to be adjusted according to individual health, activity level and reproductive status.

Breakdown

Nutrient Breakdown

Detailed analysis of the key nutrients provided by the mixed-prey diet

Protein
Essential for tissue maintenance and repair

The mixed-prey formulation provides high-quality digestible protein to support tissue maintenance and repair. Whole fish and cephalopods supply all essential amino acids required by harbour seals, with squid being particularly rich in highly digestible lean protein Mazzaro, Koutsos and Williams, 2016.

Protein requirements vary with life stage — growing pups, pregnant and lactating females have elevated needs compared to healthy adult maintenance. The combination of fish and squid ensures a complete amino acid profile while avoiding excessive protein intake, which could place unnecessary metabolic demands on the kidneys MSD Veterinary Manual, 2025.

Lipids & Energy
Primary fuel for thermoregulation and metabolism

Lipids provide the primary energy source for harbour seals, supporting thermoregulation in cold aquatic environments and fuelling normal metabolic function. Herring and mackerel are particularly rich in lipid-derived energy, providing the caloric density needed to meet daily maintenance requirements of approximately 29 MJ Gili, Meijer and Lacave, 2018.

Combining oily fish with leaner prey such as squid produces a more balanced nutrient profile than reliance on a single prey species. The energy density of the diet must be carefully managed, as excessive lipid intake can lead to obesity in captive animals with reduced activity levels compared to wild counterparts Mazzaro, Koutsos and Williams, 2016.

Omega-3 Fatty Acids
Essential for cardiovascular and immune health

Omega-3 fatty acids (EPA and DHA) are abundant in oily fish such as herring and mackerel. These essential fatty acids play critical roles in cardiovascular function, immune response, anti-inflammatory processes and neurological health MSD Veterinary Manual, 2025.

However, the high concentration of polyunsaturated fatty acids in oily fish makes them susceptible to oxidative deterioration during frozen storage. This is one of the key reasons why vitamin E supplementation is essential in captive diets — it protects these valuable fatty acids from oxidation and prevents the development of rancidity that could compromise nutritional quality Mazzaro, Koutsos and Williams, 2016.

Vitamins
Fat-soluble and water-soluble micronutrients

Whole fish provide a range of fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) and water-soluble B-complex vitamins. However, the freezing, storage and thawing processes significantly reduce the concentration and bioavailability of several key vitamins, particularly thiamine (B₁) and vitamin E Mazzaro, Koutsos and Williams, 2016; MSD Veterinary Manual, 2025.

Additionally, oily fish such as herring contain thiaminase, an enzyme that actively degrades thiamine. Without routine supplementation, this can lead to serious deficiency states with neurological consequences MSD Veterinary Manual, 2025; Croft et al., 2013. See the Vitamin Deficiencies section below for more detail.

Minerals & Micronutrients
Calcium, phosphorus, selenium and trace elements

Whole-prey diets are particularly valuable because they include skeletal tissues, organs and bones, providing a natural balance of calcium, phosphorus and trace minerals. This is a significant advantage over muscle-meat-only diets, which can produce calcium–phosphorus imbalances Gili, Meijer and Lacave, 2018.

Capelin contributes useful micronutrients including selenium and iodine, while squid provides copper and other trace elements. The variety of prey species in the proposed diet ensures a broad spectrum of mineral intake that more closely mirrors the nutritional profile of wild-caught prey Mazzaro, Koutsos and Williams, 2016.

Critical Awareness

Vitamin Deficiency Risks

Understanding the primary nutritional risks associated with frozen-thawed prey diets

Although whole fish provide the foundation of captive harbour seal diets, routine supplementation is essential because freezing, storage and thawing reduce the concentration and bioavailability of several nutrients Mazzaro, Koutsos and Williams, 2016; MSD Veterinary Manual, 2025. Two vitamins require particular attention in captive management.

Thiamine (Vitamin B₁)

Critical neurological cofactor

Particular attention should be given to thiamine, as oily fish such as herring contain thiaminase, an enzyme that degrades thiamine and can lead to deficiency if supplementation is not provided MSD Veterinary Manual, 2025.

Common clinical signs:

  • Neurological disorders and seizures
  • Anorexia and weight loss
  • Impaired coordination and ataxia
  • Lethargy and behavioural changes

Croft et al., 2013

Vitamin E

Lipid-soluble antioxidant

Vitamin E supplementation is also recommended because the high concentration of polyunsaturated fatty acids in oily fish is susceptible to oxidative deterioration during frozen storage Mazzaro, Koutsos and Williams, 2016.

Key functions compromised:

  • Cell membrane protection from oxidative damage
  • Immune function suppression
  • Reproductive performance decline
  • Steatitis (inflammation of fat tissue)

Mazzaro, Koutsos and Williams, 2016

Clinical Significance

Thiamine deficiency has been associated with neurological disorders, anorexia and impaired coordination in captive marine mammals Croft et al., 2013. Because clinical signs can develop rapidly and may be irreversible if not addressed promptly, routine prophylactic supplementation is considered a standard of care in zoological collections MSD Veterinary Manual, 2025.

Protocol

Supplementation Protocol

A systematic approach to maintaining nutritional adequacy in frozen-thawed prey diets

Routine supplementation with thiamine and vitamin E should form an integral part of captive dietary management to maintain nutritional adequacy and minimise the risk of deficiency associated with long-term feeding of frozen-thawed prey Mazzaro, Koutsos and Williams, 2016; MSD Veterinary Manual, 2025.

The Supplementation Process

Four essential steps to ensure nutritional adequacy in every meal

1

Thaw

Safely thaw frozen prey at controlled refrigeration temperatures to minimise bacterial growth and nutrient loss

2

Supplement

Administer thiamine (B₁) and vitamin E supplements according to veterinary protocol at each feeding session

3

Verify

Confirm supplement consumption through target feeding and record individual intake in daily logs

4

Monitor

Track body condition, blood parameters and clinical signs to adjust supplementation as needed

Management

Feeding Strategy & Nutritional Monitoring

Structured feeding schedules and behavioural enrichment to support natural foraging behaviour

The daily ration should be divided into three to five feeding sessions to better reflect the opportunistic feeding behaviour of wild harbour seals and reduce prolonged fasting periods Gili, Meijer and Lacave, 2018. In the wild, harbour seals spend considerable time locating and capturing prey, whereas captive individuals consume prepared diets within a relatively short period.

Therefore, feeding should incorporate target feeding, scatter feeding and environmental enrichment to encourage natural foraging behaviours, increase feeding duration and provide behavioural stimulation. Target feeding also facilitates voluntary participation in husbandry procedures, including health examinations and weighing, while ensuring that each animal receives its allocated ration Gili, Meijer and Lacave, 2018.

Routine Monitoring Records

Routine nutritional monitoring is essential to evaluate the effectiveness of the feeding programme. Records should include daily food intake, supplement consumption, body weight, body condition score and feeding behaviour, as changes in appetite are often among the earliest indicators of disease in pinnipeds Gili, Meijer and Lacave, 2018. Regular veterinary assessments allow ration size and prey composition to be adjusted according to age, reproductive status, activity level and seasonal energy demands.

This food chain illustrates why harbour seals are exposed to bioaccumulation of certain nutrients and contaminants — they occupy a high trophic level, accumulating the nutritional (and potentially toxicological) profile of all prey consumed throughout their lives. This ecological reality underscores the importance of sourcing high-quality prey species and monitoring the health of captive animals through regular veterinary assessment Gili, Meijer and Lacave, 2018.

Analysis

Critical Evaluation

A balanced assessment of the strengths and limitations of the proposed mixed-prey diet

The proposed mixed-prey diet provides a balanced approach to meeting the nutritional and behavioural requirements of captive harbour seals by supplying complementary sources of energy, protein and essential fatty acids while reflecting the species' naturally varied feeding ecology Gili, Meijer and Lacave, 2018. Dietary diversity also reduces dependence on a single prey species, thereby minimising the effects of seasonal variation in nutrient composition and improving the overall resilience of the feeding programme.

Strengths

  • Provides complementary sources of energy, protein and essential fatty acids
  • Reflects the naturally varied feeding ecology of wild harbour seals Gili, Meijer and Lacave, 2018
  • Reduces dependence on a single prey species
  • Minimises effects of seasonal variation in nutrient composition
  • Improves overall resilience of the feeding programme
  • Supports behavioural enrichment through prey variety

Limitations

  • Nutritional quality of frozen-thawed prey is influenced by storage duration and handling practices Mazzaro, Koutsos and Williams, 2016
  • Consistent feed quality is difficult to maintain
  • Increased reliance on vitamin supplementation
  • Commercial availability, seasonal fluctuations and cost may restrict provision of preferred prey species
  • Published nutrient requirements for harbour seals remain limited Gili, Meijer and Lacave, 2018
  • Many feeding recommendations based on husbandry experience rather than controlled nutritional studies
Evidence Gap

Although mixed-prey diets are widely recommended, the absence of controlled nutritional trials means that many husbandry recommendations remain based on expert consensus rather than species-specific experimental evidence. Consequently, successful dietary management requires a flexible, evidence-based approach in which feeding practices are continually refined through veterinary monitoring, body condition assessment and emerging research to ensure long-term health and welfare Gili, Meijer and Lacave, 2018.
