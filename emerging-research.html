Can Problem-Solving Toys Ease Seal Pups' Stress in Rehab?

A new study from The Marine Mammal Center examines whether cognitive enrichment reduces anticipatory behavior in rehabilitating harbour seal pups

Published 2025
14 Seal Pups
The Marine Mammal Center, California
Animals, 15(22), 3237

Study Overview

If you've ever worked in a marine mammal rehab centre, you know the tension: sterile, hygienic enclosures keep pups safe from disease and habituation to humans, but they're also boring — and boredom has a welfare cost. A new open-access study from The Marine Mammal Center in California asks a deceptively simple question: does the type of enrichment you give a rehabilitating harbour seal pup change how "wound up" it gets waiting for its next meal?

Methods

How the researchers measured emotional state without touching the animals

Anticipatory Behavior

Researchers observed spy-hopping, pacing at the gate, and alert-staring at the door in the minutes before scheduled feeds, cued by a one-minute spray from a garden hose.

Cognitive Enrichment

Puzzle feeders including hinged feed boxes and fish-stuffed "kelp forest" devices that pups had to manipulate to extract a herring reward.

Structural Enrichment

Barrels, buoys, and floating platforms — physical objects to push, swim through, or haul out on during daily enrichment sessions.

Enrichment Dose Score

A continuous score tracking how much of each seal's rehabilitation time skewed toward cognitive versus structural devices, accounting for real-world enclosure constraints.

The team followed fourteen stranded Eastern Pacific harbour seal pups through two rehabilitation seasons, splitting their daily enrichment sessions into two flavours: "structural" enrichment (barrels, buoys, floating platforms — things to push, swim through or haul out on) and "cognitive" enrichment (puzzle feeders like a hinged feed box or a fish-stuffed "kelp forest" that pups had to manipulate to extract a herring reward).

To measure emotional state without touching the animals, researchers watched for anticipatory behaviour — spy-hopping, pacing at the gate, alert-staring at the door — in the minutes before a scheduled feed, cued by a one-minute spray from a garden hose. More intense or prolonged anticipatory behaviour is thought to reflect a stronger, more urgent "need" for the upcoming reward.

Because pool availability limited strict randomisation, the team instead built a continuous "enrichment dose" score for each seal, tracking how much of their rehabilitation time skewed toward cognitive versus structural devices.

The headline finding: simply interacting more with enrichment, of either kind, didn't change anticipatory behaviour. But which type of enrichment a pup mostly received did. Seals whose enrichment leaned cognitive tended to show shorter bouts of anticipatory behaviour before feeds than seals who mostly got structural enrichment — a trend consistent with prior zoo research linking cognitive challenge to lower "reward urgency" and, by extension, better emotional regulation.

Structural enrichment kept seals engaged in the moment (interaction time was actually similar between groups: 22.8% vs 20.7% of session time), but it may not have satisfied the same underlying need that a solvable problem does.

Key Findings

What this research tells us about enrichment and welfare

Cognitive Challenge Matters

Seals receiving primarily cognitive enrichment showed shorter anticipatory behavior bouts, suggesting better emotional regulation and lower "reward urgency."

Interaction Time Similar

Both enrichment types achieved similar engagement levels (22.8% vs 20.7%), but cognitive enrichment provided different welfare benefits beyond mere interaction.

Low-Cost Welfare Metric

Anticipatory behavior is a genuinely low-cost welfare metric — no blood draws, no handling, just a camera and a feeding cue — applicable across pinniped facilities.

Not All Enrichment Equal

Puzzle-style, food-motivated cognitive devices may deserve priority over purely structural toys, particularly for young or stress-sensitive individuals.

Individual Variation

One seal, Sionna, showed high anticipatory spy-hopping regardless of enrichment dose, reminding us that welfare indicators need interpreting at the individual level.

Emotional Regulation

Cognitive enrichment appears to satisfy an underlying need that structural toys don't, promoting calmer, more emotionally regulated animals in rehabilitation.

Study Limitations

  • Small sample size: Only 14 pups across two rehabilitation seasons
  • Limited randomisation: Enrichment type wasn't fully randomised due to real-world enclosure constraints and pool availability
  • Individual quirks: Individual variation mattered — one seal (Sionna) showed high anticipatory spy-hopping regardless of enrichment dose
  • No physiological markers: Study relied solely on behavioral observation without blood draws or stress hormone measurements
  • Short-term focus: Did not assess whether cognitive enrichment translates into better post-release survival

Future Research Directions

The logical next steps to build on these findings

Larger Controlled Trial

A larger, better-controlled trial with strict randomisation to confirm whether cognitive enrichment's edge holds up across more individuals and facilities.

Physiological Markers

Pair behavioral observations with physiological stress markers (cortisol, heart rate variability) to validate anticipatory behavior as a welfare indicator.

Post-Release Survival

Track whether cognitive enrichment during rehabilitation translates into better post-release survival — ultimately what rehab is for.

Multi-Species Application

Test whether findings apply to other pinniped species and age classes in both rehabilitation and permanent captive settings.

Practical Implications

What this means for keepers, curators, and rehabilitation facilities

Prioritize Cognitive Devices

If the goal is calmer, more emotionally regulated animals, puzzle-style, food-motivated cognitive devices may deserve priority over purely structural toys.

Focus on Vulnerable Individuals

Particularly important for young or stress-sensitive individuals who may benefit most from cognitive challenge and emotional regulation support.

Daily Monitoring Tool

Anticipatory behavior could be folded into daily monitoring almost anywhere pinnipeds are housed, not just in rehabilitation settings.

Individual Assessment

Welfare indicators need interpreting at the individual as well as the group level — what works for most may not work for all.

For keepers and curators, this matters for a very practical reason: anticipatory behaviour is a genuinely low-cost welfare metric — no blood draws, no handling, just a camera and a feeding cue — that could be folded into daily monitoring almost anywhere pinnipeds are housed, not just in rehab.

And it suggests that not all enrichment is functionally equal: if the goal is calmer, more emotionally regulated animals, puzzle-style, food-motivated cognitive devices may deserve priority over purely structural toys, particularly for young or stress-sensitive individuals.

The authors are candid about the limits here: the sample was small (14 pups), enrichment type wasn't fully randomised due to real-world enclosure constraints, and individual quirks mattered — one seal, Sionna, showed high anticipatory spy-hopping regardless of her enrichment dose, a reminder that welfare indicators need interpreting at the individual as well as the group level.

The logical next step is a larger, better-controlled trial, ideally paired with physiological stress markers, to confirm whether cognitive enrichment's edge holds up — and whether it translates into better post-release survival, which is ultimately what rehab is for.
