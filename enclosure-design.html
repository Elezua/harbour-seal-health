Foundation

Enclosure Design as Preventative Health Strategy

Moving beyond architecture to view enclosure design as an active healthcare intervention

Enclosure design should be regarded as a preventative healthcare intervention in its own right rather than merely an architectural or aesthetic consideration. Whereas veterinary treatment and routine husbandry largely respond to health problems after animals have been exposed to risk, well-designed facilities proactively eliminate many of the environmental conditions that promote disease, injury and chronic stress, reflecting the principles of the Five Domains framework, which integrates nutrition, environment, physical health and behaviour to optimise overall welfare Mellor and Beausoleil, 2015.

For harbour seals, this requires replicating key features of their natural ecology, particularly access to both aquatic and terrestrial environments, as haul-out behaviour, thermoregulation and appropriate social spacing are fundamental to normal physiological and behavioural function Gili, Meijer and Lacave, 2018. Enclosures that fail to meet these ecological requirements may simultaneously increase the risk of dermatological and ocular disease, behavioural abnormalities and reduced reproductive performance, creating interconnected welfare challenges that are difficult to resolve through veterinary intervention alone Mason, 2010.

The Preventative Value of Design

Investment in evidence-based enclosure design offers greater long-term preventative value than reliance on reactive clinical management, because it removes health risks at their source while supporting sustainable welfare, reproductive success and collection resilience.

Critical Component

Water Quality & Aquatic Environment

The most critical component of preventative health management, influencing dermatological, ocular, respiratory, gastrointestinal and immune function

Water quality is arguably the most critical component of preventative health management for captive harbour seals because it simultaneously influences dermatological, ocular, respiratory, gastrointestinal and immune function. Unlike many enclosure features that affect only specific aspects of welfare, deterioration in water quality has collection-wide consequences because every animal is continuously exposed to the aquatic environment.

Pools should therefore provide sufficient depth and volume to permit full submersion and natural diving behaviour; NSW regulatory standards recommend a minimum volume of approximately 200 m³ for a small group, an average depth of 2.0 m and a diving column of at least 2.5 m, with dimensions increased according to group size NSW Department of Primary Industries, 2008. Continuous mechanical and biological filtration, supported by protein skimming and UV or ozone sterilisation, removes organic waste and reduces bacterial and viral loads, while maintaining appropriate salinity is essential because inadequate filtration and unstable water chemistry are strongly associated with keratopathy and dermatological disease Colitz et al., 2019.

Collection-Wide Impact

Maintaining marine salinity between approximately 26–36 ppt, pH between 7.2 and 8.4, and temperatures generally below 26 °C preserves normal osmotic balance and minimises thermal stress NSW Department of Primary Industries, 2008. High water clarity and adequate turnover rates further facilitate behavioural observation while limiting pathogen accumulation and disease transmission across the collection Siebert et al., 2024.

Long-Term Preventative Value

Investment in robust water-management systems is likely to deliver greater long-term preventative health benefits than many reactive veterinary interventions, as effective water quality management removes multiple disease risks simultaneously rather than addressing their clinical consequences after they occur.

Infrastructure

Physical Enclosure Design

The first line of preventative healthcare — eliminating structural hazards before they result in injury

Physical enclosure design functions as the first line of preventative healthcare by eliminating structural hazards before they result in injury, thereby reducing dependence on reactive veterinary treatment. Haul-out platforms and dry resting areas should occupy a substantial proportion of the enclosure and incorporate non-slip, gently sloping substrates that enable effortless water exit, minimise slipping injuries and support natural resting behaviour NSW Department of Primary Industries, 2008.

Adequate shade and shelter are equally essential, as prolonged exposure to solar radiation increases the risk of thermal stress and ocular disease while reducing opportunities for behavioural thermoregulation Clegg and Butterworth, 2017. Sufficient haul-out space per individual, together with multiple water-access points, reduces crowding, social competition and dominance-related trauma, allowing seals greater behavioural choice and social flexibility.

Behavioural Health

Environmental Enrichment & Behavioural Design

An essential component of preventative healthcare that reduces chronic stress and promotes species-typical behaviours

Environmental enrichment should be regarded as an essential component of preventative healthcare rather than a welfare enhancement alone, because chronic stress produces measurable physiological effects, including immunosuppression, dysregulated cortisol secretion and increased susceptibility to infectious and non-infectious disease. Increasing environmental complexity through variable swimming routes, manipulable objects, foraging devices and sensory stimuli promotes species-typical behaviours while reducing abnormal repetitive behaviours; in captive common seals, structured environmental enrichment significantly decreased stereotypic circling, demonstrating a direct relationship between enclosure design and behavioural health Grindrod and Cleaver, 2001.

Scatter- and puzzle-feeding extend natural foraging time, increase activity levels and reduce obesity associated with predictable feeding schedules. Positive reinforcement training areas facilitate voluntary participation in veterinary procedures, thereby reducing handling stress and improving preventative health monitoring, while carefully managed social groupings minimise isolation without increasing aggression. Providing animals with choice and control through multiple resting, swimming and retreat areas enhances adaptive coping and positive welfare states, consistent with the Five Domains framework Mellor and Beausoleil, 2015.

Dynamic, Not Static

Enrichment should be continually reviewed and varied because predictable or static enrichment rapidly loses behavioural value through habituation. Behavioural design should be recognised as a dynamic preventative health intervention that strengthens resilience, reduces chronic disease risk and supports long-term physical and psychological wellbeing.

Disease Prevention

Biosecurity & Enclosure Management

Engineering biosecurity into enclosure infrastructure for reliable, sustainable disease prevention

Enclosure design fundamentally determines biosecurity effectiveness and should therefore be regarded as an integral component of preventative health management rather than a separate architectural consideration. Dedicated keeper access routes and equipment assigned exclusively to quarantine, hospital and resident animal areas minimise cross-contamination, consistent with EAZA/EAAM biosecurity guidance for pinnipeds Gili, Meijer and Lacave, 2018.

Physically separate quarantine pools with independent filtration and drainage systems are essential to prevent pathogen transfer during isolation and safeguard the resident collection Meehan, 2012. Footbaths at controlled access points, drainage systems directing waste away from animal areas and surfaces designed for rapid cleaning and disinfection collectively reduce faecal-oral and water-borne transmission, as inadequate sanitation permits persistent environmental contamination Siebert et al., 2024.

Engineered, Not Just Managed

Biosecurity is most effective when engineered into enclosure infrastructure because reliance on staff compliance alone is inherently vulnerable to human error. Incorporating quarantine separation, controlled access and hygienic drainage during the design stage therefore creates a more resilient, sustainable and reliable disease-prevention system than retrofitting these measures after construction.

Ongoing Care

Environmental Monitoring & Maintenance

Systematic monitoring that transforms enclosure design into an active preventative health strategy

Systematic environmental monitoring transforms enclosure design into an active preventative health strategy by identifying environmental and mechanical failures before they compromise animal welfare. Daily procedures should include testing water quality parameters such as pH, salinity and temperature, alongside visual inspections of the enclosure, filtration systems and animal behaviour, with routine cleaning of haul-out areas and pool surrounds to minimise pathogen accumulation NSW Department of Primary Industries, 2008.

Weekly inspections of pumps, valves, barriers and other critical infrastructure enable early identification of mechanical deterioration before system failure disrupts environmental conditions, while monthly servicing of filtration, UV and ozone treatment systems maintains optimal water quality and disease control Gili, Meijer and Lacave, 2018. Annual infrastructure audits should evaluate structural integrity, drainage efficiency and biosecurity zoning against current husbandry standards, ensuring facilities remain effective as operational demands and best-practice guidance evolve Meehan, 2012.

Continuous Feedback System

Crucially, monitoring should function as a continuous feedback system rather than a routine checklist, allowing management decisions to be adapted proactively. This preventative approach shifts enclosure management from reactive maintenance to evidence-based risk reduction, ensuring that environmental conditions consistently support the long-term health, welfare and resilience of the collection.

Editorial Analysis

Beyond Compliance: The True Measure of Enclosure Design

Ultimately, enclosure design should not be evaluated solely against minimum regulatory standards; its true effectiveness lies in the extent to which it proactively prevents disease, injury and chronic stress before veterinary or behavioural intervention becomes necessary.

Integrating water quality, physical infrastructure, biosecurity, environmental enrichment and ongoing maintenance into a single adaptive management system, rather than treating them as isolated design features, determines whether an enclosure simply meets compliance requirements or actively promotes long-term health, welfare and resilience in captive harbour seals.
