Foundation

Preventative Health Priorities

A structured, risk-based approach to prioritising hazards in captive harbour seal management

Preventative health management is fundamental to the long-term welfare, longevity and reproductive success of captive harbour seals because many health problems are more effectively prevented than treated after clinical disease develops Gili, Meijer and Lacave, 2018. Consequently, preventative programmes should adopt a structured risk-based approach, prioritising hazards according to both their likelihood of occurrence and the potential consequences for individual animals, the wider collection and human health Ahl et al., 1993.

Based on these criteria, the principal preventative health priorities include infectious disease introduction, non-infectious health problems, water quality, nutritional management and chronic stress. Infectious diseases represent the highest priority because pathogens such as phocine distemper virus can spread rapidly through captive populations, resulting in substantial morbidity, mortality and operational disruption if biosecurity fails Siebert et al., 2024; Härkönen et al., 2006.

Why Prevention Outweighs Treatment

Unlike nutritional or behavioural disorders, which generally develop gradually and affect individuals, infectious disease outbreaks and water-quality failures can compromise an entire collection within a short period, making them the most cost-effective targets for preventative intervention.

Water quality is therefore similarly prioritised because inadequate filtration or inappropriate water parameters compromise skin, ocular and immune health while increasing susceptibility to opportunistic infections Colitz et al., 2019. Nutritional deficiencies, including thiamine depletion associated with thiaminase-containing fish Geraci, 1972, together with chronic stress that may suppress immune function and impair welfare Mason, 2010, are generally lower-risk but require continuous monitoring because they can predispose animals to secondary health problems.

Resource Allocation Principle

An effective preventative health programme should allocate resources according to risk while remaining sufficiently adaptive to respond to changing environmental conditions, emerging disease threats and the health status of individual animals.

Team Structure

Preventative Health by Staff Roles

Three complementary roles working together to deliver coordinated, evidence-based preventative healthcare

Effective preventative health management requires coordinated effort across three key staff roles. Each contributes distinct expertise, with keepers providing continuous observation, curators delivering strategic oversight and veterinarians supplying clinical leadership. Together, they ensure that preventative healthcare is proactive, coordinated and responsive to the long-term health and welfare needs of the collection.

Keepers

First Line of Defence

Keepers represent the first line of preventative healthcare because they have the most frequent and sustained contact with harbour seals, enabling them to detect subtle deviations from normal behaviour before clinical disease develops Young and Cipreste, 2004.

  • Daily monitoring of appetite and food intake
  • Confirming consumption of dietary supplements
  • Recording swimming patterns, haul-out frequency and respiratory rate
  • Observing social interactions and participation in husbandry training
  • Routine visual inspection of skin, eyes and body condition
  • Maintaining enclosure hygiene and prompt waste removal
  • Daily monitoring of water temperature, salinity and filtration
Daily monitoring Continuous observation

Why Keepers Are Critical

Because keepers observe animals continuously, their ability to recognise subtle changes often determines whether preventative intervention occurs before disease progresses to a stage requiring intensive veterinary treatment.

Young and Cipreste, 2004

Curators

Strategic Oversight

Curators convert daily husbandry observations into collection-wide preventative health strategies by reviewing health records, water-quality reports and preventative medicine schedules to identify emerging trends and ensure consistent standards of care National Research Council, 2004.

  • Reviewing health records and water-quality reports
  • Identifying emerging health trends across the collection
  • Overseeing biosecurity and quarantine compliance
  • Implementing EAZA and EAAM husbandry guidelines
  • Coordinating staff training and competency assessments
  • Managing emergency preparedness protocols
  • Translating observations into coordinated management decisions
Weekly/ongoing Strategic review

Strategic Coordination

This strategic oversight ensures that isolated husbandry observations are translated into coordinated management decisions, reducing the likelihood that recurring health issues remain undetected across the collection.

Gili, Meijer and Lacave, 2018

Veterinarians

Clinical Leadership

Veterinarians provide clinical oversight through routine physical examinations, diagnostic blood screening, parasite surveillance and review of vaccination protocols to identify disease before clinical signs become apparent Meehan, 2012.

  • Routine physical examinations and diagnostic screening
  • Haematology and serum biochemistry interpretation
  • Parasite surveillance and faecal screening
  • Review and development of vaccination protocols
  • Training voluntary husbandry behaviours for procedures
  • Integrating clinical findings with keeper and curator input
  • Evidence-based decision-making for disease prevention
Scheduled/periodic Clinical oversight

Modern Clinical Practice

Increasingly, procedures are performed through trained voluntary husbandry behaviours rather than physical restraint, reducing stress while improving the quality of clinical assessments.

Martelli and Krishnasamy, 2023

Infectious Disease

Major Infectious Disease Risks

Understanding the pathogens that pose the greatest threat to captive harbour seal collections

Infectious disease prevention is a major priority in captive harbour seal management because pathogens can spread rapidly through a collection, compromising animal welfare, biosecurity and conservation programmes. Phocine distemper virus (PDV) is considered the greatest infectious threat due to its efficient respiratory transmission and history of causing mass mortality during the 1988 and 2002 North Sea epizootics, which killed more than 20,000 and 30,000 harbour seals respectively Härkönen et al., 2006.

Its capacity for rapid collection-wide transmission means that preventing pathogen introduction through quarantine and biosecurity is considerably more effective than attempting to control an outbreak after clinical disease becomes established. Influenza A viruses also pose a significant risk because they can be transmitted through respiratory droplets and contaminated environments, with some strains demonstrating adaptation to marine mammals Anthony et al., 2012.

Rapid Transmission Risk

Emerging influenza strains may evolve more rapidly than husbandry protocols. Consequently, routine respiratory monitoring, minimising contact with wild birds and participation in wildlife disease surveillance networks are important preventative measures Anthony et al., 2012.

Bacterial pathogens, including Salmonella spp. and Erysipelothrix rhusiopathiae, are primarily transmitted through contaminated food, water or faecal material and may persist within enclosures if hygiene standards are inadequate Siebert et al., 2024. Lungworm (Otostrongylus circumlitus) infection, acquired through infected prey or intermediate hosts, represents an important parasitic disease, particularly in juvenile seals, highlighting the need for routine faecal screening and parasite surveillance van Wijngaarden et al., 2021.

Although bacterial and parasitic diseases can adversely affect individual animals, they are generally more amenable to targeted hygiene measures, feed management and surveillance than highly transmissible viral infections, reinforcing the need to prioritise resources according to transmission risk rather than disease severity alone.

Occupational Health

Zoonotic Considerations

Protecting staff from pathogens that can transmit between animals and humans

Occupational Zoonotic Risks

Several pathogens present occupational risks to animal-care staff through contact with infected animals, contaminated water or biological samples Deepak et al., 2019. Protecting staff from zoonotic infection also safeguards animal health by reducing opportunities for bidirectional pathogen transmission and maintaining continuity of animal care.

Salmonella spp.

Enteric pathogen transmitted through contaminated water, fish or faecal material

Vibrio spp.

Marine bacteria associated with wound infections from contaminated water

Brucella spp.

Staff Protection Measures

  • Appropriate personal protective equipment (PPE)
  • Rigorous hand hygiene protocols
  • Safe sample handling procedures
  • Strict biosecurity protocols Bjork et al., 2024
  • Regular staff health surveillance
  • Vaccination where available
Non-Infectious

Non-Infectious Health Problems

Nutritional, physical and behavioural conditions that require the same preventative emphasis as infectious diseases

Non-infectious health conditions can significantly compromise the welfare of captive harbour seals and therefore require the same preventative emphasis as infectious diseases. Nutritional disorders include thiamine deficiency associated with thiaminase-containing fish and vitamin E deficiency resulting from oxidation of frozen-thawed prey, both of which are largely preventable through appropriate supplementation and feed-quality management Mazzaro, Koutsos and Williams, 2016.

Unlike many infectious diseases, these disorders usually develop gradually, making routine nutritional monitoring particularly effective for preventing irreversible clinical consequences. Obesity may also develop when dietary energy intake exceeds expenditure, highlighting the importance of ration adjustment based on body weight and body condition scoring MSD Veterinary Manual, 2024.

Physical health problems, including traumatic injuries and dental disease, may result from enclosure hazards, social interactions or ageing, and are minimised through safe enclosure design, husbandry training and routine veterinary examinations Kahle et al., 2018. Behavioural problems, including chronic stress, reduced activity and stereotypic behaviours, are recognised indicators of compromised welfare and can increase disease susceptibility Mason, 2010.

Environmental

Environmental Health Factors

Water quality and thermal management as foundational pillars of preventative health

Environmental factors such as poor water quality and thermal stress can impair physiological function, reduce immune competence and predispose seals to disease, demonstrating that environmental management not only prevents direct pathology but also reduces vulnerability to secondary health problems Colitz et al., 2019; Gili, Meijer and Lacave, 2018. Consequently, preventative management should integrate nutritional, environmental, veterinary and behavioural monitoring because these risk factors frequently interact.

Water Quality Management

Poor salinity and filtration are strongly linked to keratopathy and skin disease in captive pinnipeds. Daily monitoring of temperature, salinity, filtration performance and microbial load is essential to prevent ocular, dermatological and infectious diseases Colitz et al., 2019.

Quarantine & Biosecurity

A structured quarantine protocol to prevent pathogen introduction and support safe acclimatisation

Quarantine and biosecurity are fundamental components of preventative health management, preventing the introduction of infectious diseases while allowing newly acquired harbour seals to acclimatise safely to their new environment MSD Veterinary Manual, 2026. On arrival, animals should be housed in dedicated quarantine facilities with no direct contact with the resident collection and separate equipment used to minimise cross-contamination Gili, Meijer and Lacave, 2018.

A minimum isolation period of 30 days is recommended, although this should be extended where clinical findings or disease risk assessments indicate Meehan, 2012. Release into the main collection should occur only after veterinary clearance confirms satisfactory clinical findings, negative diagnostic results, stable body condition and consistent normal feeding and behavioural patterns.

Monitoring Programme

Preventative Health Monitoring

A continuous, structured process integrating behavioural, nutritional, environmental and veterinary assessments

Preventative health monitoring should be a continuous, structured process that integrates behavioural, nutritional, environmental and veterinary assessments to identify health problems before clinical disease develops, thereby supporting evidence-based husbandry and long-term welfare Gili, Meijer and Lacave, 2018. Daily monitoring by keepers should include food intake, appetite, swimming and haul-out behaviour, respiratory rate, social interactions, supplement consumption and water-quality parameters, as changes in these indicators often provide the earliest evidence of illness, stress or environmental deterioration EAZA/EAAM Husbandry Guidelines, n.d..

Weekly monitoring should include body weight, body condition scoring, behavioural welfare assessments and review of husbandry records to detect gradual changes in health and nutritional status Gulland et al., 2018. Quarterly veterinary monitoring should comprise haematological and biochemical blood screening, faecal parasite surveillance and review of preventative medicine protocols, enabling the detection of subclinical abnormalities that may not be apparent through routine observation MSD Veterinary Manual, 2023.

Integration Is Key

Critically, the effectiveness of this programme depends not simply on the frequency of monitoring but on the integration and interpretation of findings across disciplines, enabling timely, evidence-based interventions that strengthen long-term health, welfare and resilience at both individual and collection levels Gili, Meijer and Lacave, 2018.

Critical Analysis

Critical Evaluation

A balanced assessment of the integrated preventative health approach

An Integrated Approach

An effective preventative health programme relies on integrating behavioural, nutritional, environmental and veterinary monitoring into a coordinated management strategy rather than treating each component independently. This multi-layered approach facilitates early disease detection, reduces the risk of infectious disease outbreaks and minimises the development of nutrition- and husbandry-related disorders.

However, its success depends on consistent implementation, effective communication between keepers, curators and veterinarians, and adequate institutional resources. As new evidence on pinniped health and husbandry emerges, preventative protocols should be regularly reviewed and adapted to reflect current best practice.

Consequently, preventative healthcare should be regarded as a dynamic, evidence-based process that underpins long-term health, welfare and sustainable collection management.
